# Phase 5 — Complete Scoped Research Topic List

**Timeframe:** Weeks 1–32, 672 scheduled hours. This is a **research-methods curriculum plus ONE chosen mathematical specialization**. A complete list of every topic in mathematical research is impossible; the complete requirements *for this 32-week project* are below. [SCHEDULE.md](SCHEDULE.md) assigns them in order.

## A — Choosing and defining a research specialty (Weeks 1–4)

- [ ] Assess existing mathematics by testing research-level definitions, core theorems, techniques and known counterexamples.
- [ ] Select one specialty and one carefully bounded subtopic from T1–T5 below.
- [ ] Understand what counts as a mathematical problem, conjecture, theorem, proof, counterexample and computational conjecture.
- [ ] Identify three representative graduate references or survey papers and map prerequisites.
- [ ] Formulate 3–5 research questions of increasing difficulty; distinguish tractable from famous open problems.
- [ ] Record assumptions, objects, quantifiers, scope, intended mathematical tools and likely obstructions.
- [ ] Set up LaTeX, BibTeX/BibLaTeX, versioned notes and optional proof/coding environment.
- [ ] Draft an honest, editable 1–2 page specialty and project brief.

## B — Research literature and deep mathematical reading (Weeks 5–9)

- [ ] Scholarly search using arXiv, zbMATH Open, MathSciNet via university, Google Scholar/semantic search and original publisher records.
- [ ] Backward/forward citation tracing; distinguish survey, peer-reviewed research, preprint and erratum.
- [ ] Search for prior results and known counterexamples *before* asserting novelty.
- [ ] Three-pass reading: relevance scan, structural read, deep theorem/proof reconstruction.
- [ ] Notation dictionary; definition, lemma, theorem, hypothesis and dependency graph.
- [ ] Extract main contribution and compare competing formulations and proof techniques.
- [ ] Detect hidden assumptions, limiting cases, special cases and proof gaps.
- [ ] Summarize at least 12 relevant papers and study **4–6** deeply across this phase.
- [ ] Maintain complete metadata, publication versions and accurate attribution.
- [ ] Produce a 5–8 page literature map and open-question list.

## C — Reproduction and mathematical validation (Weeks 10–14)

- [ ] Select one accessible published central claim, theorem/proposition, derivation or experiment.
- [ ] Reconstruct all definitions, prerequisites, assumptions and proof dependencies.
- [ ] Re-derive supporting lemmas independently; compare your result with the original.
- [ ] Check boundary cases, counterexamples and if/how assumptions are needed.
- [ ] For computational results, re-create baselines, datasets/synthetic data assumptions, randomness, tolerances and numerical error.
- [ ] Separate exact theorem verification, symbolic manipulation, formal proof checking and statistical simulation.
- [ ] Document deviations from the published result and possible explanation.
- [ ] Produce a reproducibility/reconstruction report including complete proof or code and a limitations statement.

## D — From reading to a scoped mathematical question (Weeks 15–20)

- [ ] Generate precise, answerable variations: relax one assumption, strengthen one bound, simplify one proof, examine a boundary case.
- [ ] Classify claim as existence, uniqueness, bound, convergence, approximation, algorithmic correctness or impossibility.
- [ ] Formalize quantifiers and domain; identify known tools and references.
- [ ] Test representative examples and potential counterexamples before committing.
- [ ] Investigate mathematical obstruction or failure case; keep negative results.
- [ ] Define success criteria, proof strategy, evaluation method and fallback project scope.
- [ ] If computation is involved, define validation/held-out tests, noise, random seeds and comparison baseline.
- [ ] Draft proposal: objective, closest prior work, assumptions, method, expected evidence, failure conditions.
- [ ] Request feedback on scope from a suitable faculty member/researcher **if accessible**; do not block all progress on a reply.

## E — Original investigation / proof and experiment process (Weeks 21–26)

- [ ] Prove supporting lemmas; trace every invoked theorem to an explicit hypothesis.
- [ ] Attempt direct proof, contradiction, contrapositive, induction, variational/duality or probabilistic arguments as relevant.
- [ ] Work toy cases, degeneracies, exact computations and small finite examples.
- [ ] Build counterexample searches and rigorously validate any candidate.
- [ ] Distinguish theorem from heuristic approximation and from numerical trend.
- [ ] Use symbolic/numerical/code tools as verification aids; independently verify conclusions.
- [ ] If statistical/ML, report uncertainty, generalization assumptions, experimental design, baselines, sensitivity and reproducibility.
- [ ] If algorithmic, include correctness, upper/lower bound argument, complexity and adversarial cases.
- [ ] If pure mathematics, document explicit proof, restrictions, novel examples or structural classification.
- [ ] Keep a dated research log of failed hypotheses, dead ends, modifications and remaining gaps.
- [ ] Produce at least one well-supported result: a proven statement, rigorous counterexample, faithful reproduction or carefully bounded negative finding. This need not be globally novel.

## F — Mathematical writing, review and research communication (Weeks 27–30)

- [ ] Draft paper structure: abstract, introduction, related work, preliminaries, main results, proofs/methods, discussion, limitations and references.
- [ ] Write honest claim language: proven, empirically supported, reproduced, inconclusive, open.
- [ ] Arrange definitions/lemmas/theorems logically; eliminate circular reasoning and hidden assumptions.
- [ ] Correct LaTeX notation, figures, algorithms, tables and bibliographic records.
- [ ] Re-run proofs and experiments from fresh notes/environment; reproduce tables/figures.
- [ ] Give a talk, reading-group report or recorded presentation.
- [ ] Obtain outside feedback if possible, respond with explicit corrections.
- [ ] Understand preprints, journal/conference peer review, research integrity and authorship norms.
- [ ] Revise toward a self-contained, readable manuscript.

## G — Final work and longer-term research pathway (Weeks 31–32)

- [ ] Deliver **12–20 page research-style report** with attributions and evidence.
- [ ] Reproducibility appendix or full proof appendix with assumptions and verification.
- [ ] Library of the most important 10–20 definitions/lemmas/counterexamples.
- [ ] Clearly label what is established, what is known, what may be new and what remains unresolved.
- [ ] Deliver next-stage **6–12 month** plan for deeper reading and work.
- [ ] Decide with informed feedback whether an extended project, seminar, supervised research or publication attempt is appropriate.
- [ ] Final evaluation: mathematical correctness, literature competence, rigor, independent reasoning, communication.

## Choose exactly ONE mathematical research content track

The shared A–G research topics above are **required for everyone**. The items below describe what mathematical theory you may **select** and deepen over Weeks 1–26. **Do not stack five complete specialty tracks into 672 hours.** Choose a small, defensible subset in your specialty and connect it to one narrow project.

### T1 — Mathematical machine learning / optimization
- [ ] Choose ONE: generalization and stability; nonasymptotic high-dimensional statistics; accelerated/proximal optimization; overparameterized models; diffusion mathematics (SDE prerequisites).
- [ ] Uniform convergence, Rademacher complexity or algorithmic stability as relevant.
- [ ] Concentration inequalities and random matrix techniques (selected).
- [ ] Smooth/strongly convex optimization, nonconvex critical points, or stochastic gradients (selected).
- [ ] Prove/derive one bounded result with explicit distributions, losses and parameter assumptions.
- [ ] Validate a toy or benchmark experiment with uncertainty and baseline where appropriate.
- **Example:** reconstruct a selected convergence theorem; test tightness on quadratics or a carefully specified stochastic model.

### T2 — Algorithms and theoretical computer science
- [ ] Choose ONE: randomized algorithms; approximation bounds; graph algorithms; online/streaming methods; selected complexity reductions.
- [ ] Precise machine/model of computation and input distribution where required.
- [ ] Correctness invariants, combinatorial/probabilistic counting and asymptotic bounds.
- [ ] Construct worst-case or adversarial instances; understand reduction direction.
- [ ] Reconstruct a published performance/approximation guarantee.
- [ ] Investigate a constrained instance family or special graph/data regime.
- **Example:** prove a special-case performance bound and compare to a standard algorithm.

### T3 — Probability and stochastic mathematics
- [ ] Choose ONE: concentration and martingales; stochastic-process hitting times; Markov-chain mixing; SDE numerical error; high-dimensional random matrices.
- [ ] Measurability, integrability, independence/martingale or mixing assumptions.
- [ ] Bound/limit inequalities and convergence modes.
- [ ] Explicit probabilistic examples, coupling or stopping-time arguments where suitable.
- [ ] Use simulation only to check precise model predictions; report Monte Carlo uncertainty.
- **Example:** reproduce a concentration or convergence bound for an explicit restricted process.

### T4 — Analysis, functional analysis or partial differential equations
- [ ] Choose ONE: weak elliptic PDEs; Fourier convergence; stability of numerical PDE discretizations; operator theory; nonlinear ODE/PDE examples.
- [ ] Function spaces, norms, regularity, boundary conditions and operator domain.
- [ ] A priori estimates, existence/uniqueness and approximations in a simple setting.
- [ ] Counterexamples when assumptions (smoothness, compactness, coercivity) are weakened.
- [ ] Verify analytical findings numerically where valid and feasible.
- **Example:** prove a model energy estimate and compare a discretization's observed convergence.

### T5 — Pure algebra, number theory or geometry
- [ ] Choose ONE: finite-group actions; explicit ring/finite-field structures; modular equations; combinatorial number theory; manifolds/metric geometry examples.
- [ ] Select a clearly stated family of mathematical objects or transformations.
- [ ] Establish structural lemmas, classify cases and test sharpness.
- [ ] Use computer algebra for finite examples, then distinguish evidence from proof.
- [ ] Compare classification/lemmas to established literature and document rediscoveries.
- **Example:** classify a small family of algebraic structures under explicit conditions or show a counterexample to an overbroad conjecture.

## Research standards and limits

- **Reproduction is not novelty**. Claims that a result is new require credible prior-art checks and expert review.
- **A preprint is not the same as peer review**; venue conventions matter.
- **A polished expository report can be an excellent Phase 5 outcome**, especially if original research proves too ambitious.
- Research judgment, feedback and deep specialization extend beyond the fixed 32-week schedule.
