# Mathematics Routine

The goal is long-term mathematical maturity alongside university, software engineering, DSA, and occasional competitive problem solving. The routine must be sustainable.

## Weekly time

### Standard target: 5–7 hours

- **2 concept sessions:** 75–90 minutes each;
- **2 problem sessions:** 60–90 minutes each;
- **1 review/problem-solving session:** 60–120 minutes.

During university exam periods, use the minimum routine below.

## Recommended weekly layout

| Day | Mathematics work | Time |
|---|---|---:|
| Monday | Learn one new section and work examples | 75–90 min |
| Wednesday | Routine exercises and correction | 60–75 min |
| Friday | Second concept section or derivation | 75–90 min |
| Sunday | Mixed review + one non-routine problem + planning | 90–120 min |

The exact days may change. Preserve the four session types.

## Minimum viable math week

Use this during exams, deadlines, illness, or burnout risk:

- [ ] one 45-minute concept review;
- [ ] five carefully solved exercises;
- [ ] one old mistake re-solved without notes;
- [ ] ten minutes updating the tracker.

Never turn one difficult week into a three-month disappearance.

## Session formats

### Concept session — 90 minutes

1. **5 min:** recall the previous lesson without notes.
2. **30 min:** read/watch the new concept actively.
3. **20 min:** reproduce definitions and derivations.
4. **25 min:** solve 3–6 exercises unaided.
5. **10 min:** summarize and record confusion.

### Problem session — 75 minutes

1. **10 min:** warm-up with one old question.
2. **50 min:** solve planned problems without looking at solutions.
3. **10 min:** inspect solutions and correct reasoning.
4. **5 min:** add mistakes to the error log.

### Hard-problem session — 90 minutes

1. Work for 45–60 minutes before seeking help.
2. Write examples, patterns, failed attempts, and partial claims.
3. Take one small hint rather than reading the full solution.
4. Complete the argument independently when possible.
5. Rewrite the clean solution the following day.

## Monthly cycle

### Weeks 1–3

- learn new material;
- complete routine exercises;
- solve selected harder problems;
- maintain the error log.

### Week 4

- no major new topic unless ahead;
- re-solve representative questions;
- take a 60–90 minute cumulative test;
- repair weak prerequisites;
- complete one mathematical coding notebook or written explanation.

## Daily micro-review — optional 10 minutes

Use flashcards or a notebook for:

- definitions;
- theorem conditions;
- formulas that should be instantly available;
- common algebra/trig identities;
- mistakes and counterexamples.

Do not use flashcards for entire proofs or substitute them for problem solving.

## Mathematics notebook system

Maintain four sections:

### 1. Concepts

Definitions, diagrams, intuition, and central formulas.

### 2. Derivations and proofs

Important results reproduced in my own words.

### 3. Error log

For every meaningful mistake:

- topic;
- original problem;
- incorrect thought;
- correct idea;
- why the error happened;
- date to re-solve.

### 4. Problem gems

Beautiful, reusable, or especially instructive problems.

## Mastery test before advancing

Move on when most answers are yes:

- [ ] Can I state the important definitions precisely?
- [ ] Can I solve standard exercises without notes?
- [ ] Can I explain the intuition conversationally?
- [ ] Can I derive at least the main formulas?
- [ ] Can I solve a mixed problem where the method is not announced?
- [ ] Can I connect the idea to algorithms, AI, or another topic?
- [ ] Can I still solve a representative question after one week?

Perfection is not required. Reliable recall and application are.

## Integration with DSA and competitive programming

When a topic overlaps with programming:

- implement it in C++ after understanding the mathematics;
- prove correctness before optimizing;
- record time and space complexity;
- solve 2–5 tagged problems;
- revisit the mathematical idea outside contest pressure.

Useful overlaps include:

- modular arithmetic;
- combinatorics;
- probability;
- recurrences;
- graph theory;
- logarithms and asymptotics;
- geometry;
- matrices and linear recurrences.

## Integration with AI

Beginning around Month 12, add one small numerical notebook every month. Beginning around Month 20, add one derivation from machine learning every two weeks.

Examples:

- gradient of mean squared error;
- normal equation;
- logistic loss;
- softmax;
- Bayes classifier;
- PCA;
- backpropagation;
- regularization.

## Weekly checklist

### Learning

- [ ] Completed the planned sections.
- [ ] Reproduced at least one derivation or proof.
- [ ] Solved routine exercises accurately.

### Problem solving

- [ ] Attempted at least one unfamiliar problem.
- [ ] Reviewed solutions only after a serious attempt.
- [ ] Updated the error log.
- [ ] Re-solved one previous mistake.

### Retention

- [ ] Reviewed last week's material.
- [ ] Explained one concept without notes.
- [ ] Identified next week's exact target.

## Example weekly goal

> By Sunday, I will finish logarithm laws and exponential equations, solve 20 mixed exercises, prove two log identities from exponent laws, re-solve three mistakes, and complete one Codeforces logarithm/complexity problem.

That is measurable. “Study math” is not.
